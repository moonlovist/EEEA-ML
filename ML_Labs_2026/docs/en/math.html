1. Mean absolute error

For n observations, let e_i = y_hat_i − y_i. The absolute error is |e_i|. Adding all absolute errors and dividing by n gives MAE.

Example: y=(12,19,32), y_hat=(10,20,30). Residuals=(-2,1,-2), absolute errors=(2,1,2), sum=5, n=3, MAE=5/3 min.

The squared-error training objective and the absolute-error evaluation metric measure different quantities; both are explicitly identified in Lab 2.

2. Affine gradients and the fitted line

Let J(w,b)=(1/(2n)) Σ_i (w x_i + b − y_i)². Write r_i=w x_i+b−y_i. By the chain rule, ∂(r_i²/2)/∂w=r_i x_i and ∂(r_i²/2)/∂b=r_i. Therefore ∂J/∂w=mean(r_i x_i) and ∂J/∂b=mean(r_i).

At x=(1,2,3), y=(12,19,32), w=10,b=0: r=(-2,1,-2), J=9/6=1.5, dw=(-2+2−6)/3=−2, db=(−2+1−2)/3=−1. With η=0.1: w_new=10−0.1(−2)=10.2, b_new=0−0.1(−1)=0.1. New predictions=(10.3,20.5,30.7). New J=(1.7²+1.5²+1.3²)/6=6.83/6≈1.13833.

At a least-squares optimum, db=0 gives b=y_mean−w x_mean. Substitute into dw=0: Σ(x_i−x_mean)[w(x_i−x_mean)−(y_i−y_mean)]=0. When Σ(x_i−x_mean)²>0, w=Σ(x_i−x_mean)(y_i−y_mean)/Σ(x_i−x_mean)². For the three points: x_mean=2, y_mean=21, numerator=20, denominator=2, so w=10,b=1. The Hessian is X_designᵀ X_design/n, where X_design has columns x and 1. With nonconstant x these columns are independent, so the Hessian is positive definite and this stationary point is the unique minimum.

3. Standardisation and distance

For x=(1,3), mean=(1+3)/2=2. Population variance=[(1−2)²+(3−2)²]/2=1, so sd=1. The standardised values are (1−2)/1=−1 and (3−2)/1=1. For a constant feature, StandardScaler uses a scale factor of 1; subtracting the fitted mean yields zero on the training column.

For differences (1 visit, 100 EUR), raw squared distance=1²+100²=10001. Rescaling a coordinate changes its contribution. The choice of scale determines which kinds of differences receive more weight.

4. Binary cross-entropy: every chain-rule factor

Use column notation: X has shape (n,2), W1 (2,h), b1 (h), H (n,h), W2 (h,1), b2 scalar, z,p,y (n,1). Broadcasting adds a bias to every row. The implementation stores W2 and z as one-dimensional arrays and uses explicit broadcasting for the same outer product.

For a finite logit z, p=1/(1+exp(−z)) lies in (0,1). Differentiating gives dp/dz=exp(−z)/(1+exp(−z))²=p(1−p). With loss ℓ=−y log p−(1−y)log(1−p), dℓ/dp=−y/p+(1−y)/(1−p)=(p−y)/[p(1−p)]. Multiplying by dp/dz gives dℓ/dz=p−y. Averaging n examples gives δ=(p−y)/n.

For h=tanh(a)=(exp(a)−exp(−a))/(exp(a)+exp(−a)), the quotient rule gives dh/da=[(exp(a)+exp(−a))²−(exp(a)−exp(−a))²]/(exp(a)+exp(−a))²=1−h². Apply this identity elementwise to H=tanh(A). Thus dW2=Hᵀδ, db2=Σ_i δ_i, dA=(δW2ᵀ)⊙(1−H²), dW1=XᵀdA and db1=Σ_rows dA. Each matrix product sums the contribution from the observations. The symbol ⊙ denotes elementwise multiplication.

For numerical evaluation, log p=z−log(1+exp z) and log(1−p)=−log(1+exp z). Substitution gives ℓ=log(1+exp z)−yz. np.logaddexp(0,z) evaluates the logarithmic term stably.

With 2 inputs and h hidden units, parameters=2h+h+h+1=4h+1. At h=8 this is 33; at h=2 this is 9. Gradient checks compare every parameter derivative with centred finite differences [L(θ+ε)−L(θ−ε)]/(2ε).

5. Temperature and causal attention

The empirical continuations of a/un have counts 3 and 4. Their probabilities are 3/7 and 4/7. At T=0.5, exponent 1/T=2: p₁=(3/7)²/[(3/7)²+(4/7)²]=9/25, p₂=16/25. The observed word-pair counts stay fixed.

Attention uses Q=K=[[1,0],[0,1],[1,1]], d=2, V=[[10,0],[0,10],[10,10]]. Scores are QKᵀ/sqrt(2). At row 0, the causal mask retains the first key: weights=(1,0,0), output=(10,0). At row 1, retained scores are 0 and 1/sqrt(2). Let a=1/[1+exp(1/sqrt(2))]≈0.330238. Weights=(a,1−a,0), so output=a(10,0)+(1−a)(0,10)=(3.30238,6.69762).

At row 2, scores are (1/sqrt(2),1/sqrt(2),2/sqrt(2)). Subtracting their maximum before exponentiation leaves softmax unchanged. Normalisation gives weights≈(0.248255,0.248255,0.503490). Multiplication by V gives (7.51745,7.51745).
